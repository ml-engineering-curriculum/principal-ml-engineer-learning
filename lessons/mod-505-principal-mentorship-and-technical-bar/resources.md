## Multipliers, leverage, and the whole-org lift — chapter 2's framing canon

Chapter 2's load-bearing frame — *"multiply the effectiveness of every ML team"* — has a specific body of literature behind it.

- Liz Wiseman, *Multipliers: How the Best Leaders Make Everyone Smarter* (Harper Business, revised edition 2017). The canonical treatment of leader-as-multiplier (vs. leader-as-diminisher). The *investor* and *challenger* archetypes map most cleanly onto the IC-principal's leverage practice. The *accidental diminisher* chapter is the parent treatment of chapter 2 § *failure mode: the leverage move authored alone* and § *failure mode: the leverage move that displaces staff scope*.
- Andy Grove, *High Output Management* (Vintage, revised edition 1995). The *managerial leverage* concept — one hour of a manager's time that affects N engineers' outputs — is the lineage chapter 2's framing stands in. Chapter 3 (*Managerial Leverage*) and chapter 4 (*Meetings — The Medium of Managerial Work*) are the direct read. (The IC-principal is not a manager, but the leverage calculus transfers.)
- Richard W. Hamming, *You and Your Research* ([Bell Labs lecture, 1986](https://www.cs.virginia.edu/~robins/YouAndYourResearch.html)). The *important problems* and *courage* chapters are the lineage for chapter 2 § *selection discipline — test 1 (friction-count)*: pick the problems the org is actually carrying, not the ones you happen to find interesting. Hamming's "If you don't work on an important problem, it's unlikely you'll do important work" is the single sentence most closely aligned with chapter 2's thesis.
- Richard W. Hamming, *The Art of Doing Science and Engineering: Learning to Learn* (Stripe Press reissue, 2020; original 1997). Chapter 30 (*You and Your Research*) is the expanded essay form of the Bell Labs lecture.
